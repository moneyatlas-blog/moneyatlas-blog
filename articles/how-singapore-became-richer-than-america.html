How Singapore Became Richer Than America

Published 2026-09-30 · MoneyAtlas

Filed under: Singapore, Asia, GDP, Economics

On August 9, 1965, Singapore was kicked out of Malaysia. The island had around $500 per person, no natural resources, no army, no hinterland — just 225 square miles of swamp, slums, and a deep-water port. Its leader, Lee Kuan Yew, wept on television announcing the separation.

Sixty years later, Singapore produces $94,897 per person (World Bank, 2024, nominal US dollars) — richer than the United States at $86,170. It has the world's busiest transshipment port, one of the highest home-ownership rates on Earth, and an airport routinely voted the best in the world.

This is the greatest economic turnaround in modern history. And unlike most miracles, it came with a blueprint.

The day a country was deleted

Singapore's starting position was genuinely desperate. It imported almost all its food and water. Unemployment was around 14%. Race riots had recently torn through the streets. Most observers — including, privately, many Singaporean leaders — expected the tiny state to fail within years.

Lee Kuan Yew, prime minister from 1959 to 1990, decided that survival required three insane bets. He made all three.

Bet one: open the gates

Most newly independent countries in the 1960s chose protectionism — high tariffs, state-owned industries, suspicion of foreign capital. Singapore did the exact opposite.

Lee invited multinational corporations in with open arms: low taxes, world-class infrastructure, a disciplined workforce, and a government that kept its promises. If you were a manufacturer looking for an Asian base, Singapore made you an offer you couldn't refuse.

Critics called it selling out to foreigners. Lee called it jobs. The factories came — first labor-intensive electronics and textiles, then, as wages rose, semiconductors, pharmaceuticals, and finance. Foreign investment became the engine that pulled the entire economy forward.

Bet two: one language

Singapore's population was a mix of Chinese, Malay, and Indian communities, each with its own languages and schools. Lee made a decision that was politically explosive but economically brilliant: English would be the working language, with bilingual education policy introduced from 1966.

Every Singaporean child would learn English plus their mother tongue. The result was a workforce that could do business with the entire world — a massive advantage when American, European, and Japanese companies came calling. Language policy became trade policy, and within a generation Singaporeans were among the most fluent English speakers in Asia.

Bet three: total war on corruption

This was the bet that made the other two work. Lee understood that foreign investors would only come if contracts were honored and officials couldn't be bought. So he built one of the world's most ruthless anti-corruption systems.

The Corrupt Practices Investigation Bureau was given sweeping powers. Ministers and civil servants were paid salaries competitive with the private sector — the logic being that well-paid officials are harder to bribe. And the penalties were career-ending. Singapore today consistently ranks among the least corrupt countries on Earth.

This is the part of the Singapore story that gets glossed over, but it is the foundation. Open markets without clean government just produce oligarchs. Singapore got both.

The machine that prints money

With the three bets in place, Singapore built institutions that turned the island into a wealth machine:

  • - The port. Singapore sits on the Strait of Malacca, through which a huge share of global trade passes. The government turned geography into the world's most efficient transshipment hub.
  • - Housing. The Housing & Development Board built public housing at massive scale — today, more than 80% of residents live in HDB flats, most of them owned. Public housing became a wealth-building tool for ordinary families.
  • - Forced savings. The Central Provident Fund requires workers and employers to contribute a share of every paycheck into accounts for housing, healthcare, and retirement. Singaporeans are among the most financially prepared retirees in Asia.
  • - Changi Airport and Singapore Airlines. The state built world-class connectivity as deliberate economic strategy — every flight connection is a business opportunity.

What the miracle doesn't show

Singapore's statistics hide real pressures. It is one of the most expensive cities on Earth — housing, cars (a Toyota can cost as much as a house elsewhere thanks to certificate-of-entitlement auctions), and daily life strain ordinary budgets. Income inequality is high for a developed country, and the work culture is famously intense.

The government knows this. Massive public housing keeps shelter affordable relative to incomes, public transport is world-class and cheap, and the CPF system forces a savings discipline that leaves most Singaporeans far better prepared for old age than their American or European counterparts. The social contract is clear: the state will make you prosperous and safe, and in return it expects discipline — and deference. It is a bargain most Singaporeans have accepted, election after election, because the results are visible from every window.

Could your city copy this?

Urban planners from Kigali to Riyadh study Singapore obsessively, and Saudi Arabia's NEOM project openly borrows from its playbook. The replicable parts — clean government, English-language business environment, infrastructure first, forced savings — work anywhere with enough political will.

The hard parts are the context: Singapore is a city-state, so national policy is city policy with no rural-urban divide to manage. It industrialized during the greatest globalization wave in history, when Western companies were desperate for Asian manufacturing bases. And it had Lee Kuan Yew — a leader willing to be unpopular for decades to be proven right by history.

Still, the core insight is universal and free: a government that treats competence as ideology can transform anything. Singapore had swamps. It built the future. And it did it without oil, without an empire, and without excuses — which is precisely why the story still inspires, and unsettles, six decades later.

The dark side of the miracle

No honest account of Singapore skips the trade-offs. The same government that engineered prosperity also engineered obedience. Media is tightly managed, defamation lawsuits have bankrupted opposition figures, and the state intrudes into private life in ways Americans would find startling — from chewing gum restrictions to strict drug laws with the death penalty.

Singaporeans traded a degree of political freedom for order and prosperity. Whether that trade was worth it is one of the great debates in development economics. What is not debatable is the result: it worked, materially, beyond anyone's expectations.

Blueprint or miracle?

Could another country copy Singapore? Partly. The policies — openness to investment, English education, anti-corruption, forced savings, public housing — are all replicable in theory. What is harder to replicate is the context: a tiny city-state is easier to govern than a sprawling nation, a crisis creates unity that comfort never does, and Lee Kuan Yew's particular combination of vision and ruthlessness comes along rarely.

But the core lesson travels well: good governance is the ultimate natural resource. Singapore had nothing — no oil, no farmland, no minerals. It had a port, a location, and a government obsessed with competence. Sixty years later it is richer than America.

The $94,897 lesson: Countries don't get rich from what is under the ground. They get rich from what is between their citizens' ears — and from leaders who build systems that reward work instead of connections.
